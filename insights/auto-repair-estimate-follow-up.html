How Auto Repair Shops Can Get More Repair Estimates Approved Without Chasing Customers

A practical process for explaining repair estimates, making approvals easier, and following up without overwhelming service advisors.

By Solomon Jarman · FounderNovember 14, 20265 min read

A customer drops off a vehicle, explains a strange noise, and waits for the shop to inspect it. Your service advisor calls with the findings, leaves a message, and moves on to the next customer. An hour passes. The technician is waiting for authorization, the service bay is occupied, and by closing time nobody has heard back. The repair has not been declined, but it has not been approved either. Unanswered estimates create scheduling uncertainty and frustrating follow-up work.

A better estimate follow-up process helps customers understand their options and gives the shop a predictable way to move each repair forward.

1. Explain the estimate in a way customers can act on

A long list of parts, labor lines, and technical terms can be difficult to evaluate over the phone. Before contacting the customer, prepare a concise explanation that separates the reported concern, the inspection findings, the recommended work, and any optional items.

Service advisors should be ready to explain:

  • What the technician observed, using plain language.
  • Which work is being recommended now and why.
  • Which items are maintenance suggestions or can be discussed separately.
  • The estimated price and whether additional findings could change it.
  • What the customer should do to approve, decline, or ask a question.

Avoid pressuring someone to authorize work they do not understand. If a question needs a technician's input, say so and arrange a realistic callback rather than guessing. Clear explanations build trust even when a customer decides to postpone a repair.

2. Give customers a simple approval path

A customer may be ready to approve the work but unable to answer a call. Make it easy to respond through the communication methods your shop supports.

If you use text or email estimates, make the message specific: identify the vehicle, explain that an estimate is ready, and tell the customer how to review it and contact the shop. Use a secure link or your shop-management system's approval function where available. Do not ask customers to send payment-card details or sensitive information in an ordinary text message.

At drop-off, ask which contact method the customer prefers, confirm the details, and note a useful call window if needed.

The goal is to remove friction, not to send more messages. One clear message with a working response path is more helpful than repeated generic reminders.

3. Set a follow-up rhythm and assign ownership

Without a shared routine, one advisor may call repeatedly while another assumes the estimate is being handled. Decide who owns every pending estimate and when the next follow-up should happen.

A simple workflow might be:

  • Send or explain the estimate as soon as the inspection and pricing are ready.
  • Record when and how it was sent.
  • If there is no response, make one courteous follow-up within the timeframe your shop sets.
  • If the customer has questions, assign the next action to a named person.
  • Close the loop when the work is approved, declined, deferred, or no longer relevant.

Use your shop-management system's pending-estimate report if it provides one. Otherwise, a shared daily list can work. Review open estimates at a short morning or afternoon huddle so advisors can spot stalled approvals before they disrupt the day's schedule.

Do not treat silence as authorization. Wait for the customer's clear approval under your shop's established process before proceeding with additional work.

4. Learn why estimates are not moving forward

Not every unapproved estimate is a lost sale. A customer may need time, have a question, or arrange transportation. Record the reason when shared, without making assumptions.

At the end of each week, review a few practical measures:

  • Number of estimates presented.
  • Number approved, declined, or deferred.
  • Time between estimate delivery and customer response.
  • Follow-ups still waiting for an owner.
  • Common questions or reasons customers give for delaying.

Look for patterns by day, advisor, or repair type. If customers often ask what a line item means, improve the explanation. If advisors are tied up at the counter, adjust call coverage or divide peak-hour responsibilities. Use the information to improve the process, not pressure staff into overselling.
